\noindent Statistical methods. FAMAS [11] adapts spectrum-based fault localization (SBFL) from\par
\noindent software engineering to multi-agent failure attribution. The method parses failed execution\par
\noindent logs into structured ⟨agent, action, state⟩ triples using an LLM (Qwen2.5-72B) for log parsing,\par
\noindent then applies the Kulczynski2 suspiciousness formula with two domain-specific modifications:\par
\noindent temporal decay weighting (later actions receive higher suspiciousness) and action-frequency\par
\noindent normalization (§3–4).\par
\noindent  The method requires k=20 environment replays per failure to build the execution spectrum\par
\noindent (§5.1, confirmed). Sensitivity analysis shows monotonic accuracy improvement with replay\par
\noindent count: k=5 yields 46.0 action-level correct attributions, k=10 yields 47.6, k=15 yields 50.8,\par
\noindent and k=20 yields 54 (§5.4, Figure 4). The best agent-level accuracy is 62.07\% on handcrafted\par
\noindent multi-agent systems and 57.61\% overall in the Who\&When benchmark; the best action-level\par
\noindent accuracy is 41.38\% (handcrafted) and 29.35\% (overall) (Table 2).\par
\noindent  The method’s principal cost is replay time: on average 105 minutes per task (range 38–\par
\noindent 248 minutes), while the spectrum analysis itself completes in under one minute (§5.2). This\par
\noindent makes FAMAS the most expensive T1 method by wall-clock time, but the only one with formal\par
\noindent coverage guarantees from SBFL theory. The method degrades on very short trajectories (<11\par
\noindent steps, where the spectrum is too sparse) and very long ones (38–65 steps, where the action space\par
\noindent dilutes individual suspiciousness scores) (§6.1, Figure 6). A single benchmark (Who\&When\par
\noindent with 184 failure logs) limits generalizability (§6.2).\par
