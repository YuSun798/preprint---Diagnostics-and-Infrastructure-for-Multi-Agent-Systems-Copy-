may not exhibit the inverted slope, since they do not rely on the “recovered error” heuristic.

another. Replication with step-level attribution and LLM-judge methods remains necessary to
establish generality.


8     DRICE Evaluation Framework
Existing comparison tables in multi-agent diagnostics and serving reviews typically list systems
by method category and report headline accuracy or throughput numbers. This makes cross-
layer comparison difficult: a diagnostic method with 70% attribution accuracy and zero replay
cost is not directly comparable to an infrastructure system with 3× throughput gain and 74%

cache hit rate. We propose DRICE — Diagnostics evidence, Retention policy, Infrastructure
assumptions, Cost decomposition, Evidence depth — as a structured evaluation framework for
characterizing systems that span both layers.

8.1    Framework Dimensions
D — Diagnostics evidence. What diagnostic signal does the system consume or produce? We
assess: (a) the evidence type (execution traces, attention scores, tool-call logs, prefill NLL, or
none); (b) the capture mechanism (instrumentation, model introspection, or external logging);
and (c) the evidence availability — whether the signal is available at runtime or only post-hoc.
A system that produces diagnostic evidence as a byproduct of serving (e.g., the policy-driven
runtime’s agent-identity embeddings) receives a higher D score than one that requires a separate
diagnostic pipeline.

R — Retention policy. How does the system decide what to keep and what to discard? We
characterize the retention policy along three sub-dimensions: (a) the eviction trigger (memory
pressure, TTL expiry, explicit release, or never); (b) the retention criterion (recency, frequency,
cost-benefit, workflow-awareness, or learned); and (c) the diagnostic awareness — whether the
retention criterion considers the diagnostic value of the retained state. Among surveyed systems,
only the policy-driven runtime incorporates observability signals into eviction decisions; no
system explicitly weights diagnostic evidence retention.

I — Infrastructure assumptions. What serving-infrastructure constraints does the system
assume? We distinguish: (a) model homogeneity (single-model, multi-adapter, or multi-model);
(b) memory topology (GPU-only, CPU offload, disaggregated, or tiered); (c) multi-tenancy (ded-
icated, shared-nothing, or shared-pool); and (d) workload pattern (request-level, session-level,
workflow-level, or agent-level). Infrastructure assumptions determine whether a diagnostic
method’s evidence requirements are compatible with the serving system’s state management.

C — Cost decomposition. We decompose runtime cost into four components: (a) compute
cost (replays, model inference, signal extraction); (b) memory cost (trace storage, cache over-
head, metadata); (c) latency cost (per-request overhead, diagnostic pipeline delay); and (d) com-
munication cost (inter-node transfers, cache synchronization). This decomposition enables
identifying which cost component dominates and whether diagnostic and serving costs share
the same bottleneck.

E — Evidence depth. At what level of verification is the system’s performance characterized?
We use the three-tier coding from Section 3.3: T1 (full-text with section-level evidence), T1b
(full-text without page-level citation), and T2 (abstract-verified). Evidence depth determines
how much confidence can be placed in comparative claims.

8.3    Applying DRICE to Cross-Layer Scenarios
Beyond the per-system assessment in Table 12, DRICE identifies three actionable cross-layer
scenarios:

Scenario 1: Diagnostic-unaware eviction (majority case). Systems like SGLang and Moon-
cake score high on R (sophisticated retention policies) and I (well-characterized infrastructure
assumptions) but have null D scores. A deployment pairing SGLang with CHIEF for failure
attribution would have no mechanism to protect CHIEF’s trace inputs from eviction. DRICE
makes this gap explicit: the D–R disconnect means the diagnostic and infrastructure layers
operate independently (Tier A in the overlap classification).

Scenario 2: Observability-informed eviction (emerging). The policy-driven runtime scores
partially on both D and R: it consumes observability signals (agent identity, engine events)
and uses them to predict cache residency. This is not yet diagnostic-aware in the sense of
protecting evidence for later attribution, but the architecture demonstrates that the D–R bridge
is technically feasible. DRICE’s C dimension reveals that the metadata overhead is the binding
constraint: agent-identity embeddings and transition matrices consume memory proportional to
the number of distinct agents.

Scenario 3: Joint budget optimization (hypothetical). TOPAS scores high on R (joint
prefix-scheduling optimization under shared Bt ) but null on D. Extending TOPAS to score on
D would require adding a diagnostic-value term to the objective: max α · throughput + (1−α) ·
diag_value. DRICE’s E dimension prescribes what evidence would be needed to validate such
an extension: the retention ablation protocol (Section 7.3) provides one path, but the framework
also requires throughput measurements under the modified policy, which no existing study pro-
vides.

8.4    DRICE as a Practitioner Checklist
To illustrate how DRICE translates to actionable deployment decisions, we walk through two
contrasting configurations:

Case 1: SGLang with independent trace store. D: full OTel-style spans captured out-of-
band (step-level attribution available). R: traces stored in an independent log database, not in
GPU memory. I: RadixTree prefix cache with LRU leaf-first eviction. C: negligible diagnostic
overhead (tracing is asynchronous and does not contend with serving memory). E: T1a evidence
depth for SGLang’s eviction mechanics. Decision: no diagnostic-serving conflict exists because
R is decoupled from I. The retention policy for diagnostic traces is unconstrained by serving-
side memory pressure. This is the Tier A (independent operation) configuration that the majority
of surveyed deployments use.

Case 2: Hypothetical co-located system (TOPAS-like). D: diagnostic signals embedded in
KV cache block metadata (tool-call markers, error flags). R: shared GPU memory pool —
evicting a KV block destroys the embedded diagnostic content. I: PagedAttention with LRU
eviction under memory pressure. C: the illustrative simulation (§6.6) shows an 11.6 pp cache
hit-rate cost for complete diagnostic block retention. E: agent-level attribution only (step-level
requires additional trace infrastructure). Decision: joint optimization is needed. Either accept

8.5    Relationship to ALEMC
The DRICE framework is complementary to ALEMC (Accuracy, Latency, Energy, Memory,
Carbon) proposed for on-device RAG evaluation. Where ALEMC focuses on single-system
performance metrics with explicit sustainability dimensions, DRICE focuses on cross-system
characterization — what each system assumes about, produces for, and depends on from adja-
cent layers. The two frameworks can be composed: ALEMC dimensions apply within each
DRICE component (e.g., the C dimension’s compute cost can be further decomposed into
ALEMC’s latency and energy metrics). A system’s DRICE profile identifies which layers it
spans; ALEMC quantifies how well it performs within each layer.

8.6    Limitations of DRICE
DRICE is a characterization framework, not a ranking system. It does not produce a single score
because the dimensions are incommensurable: a high D score (rich evidence consumption) is
not inherently better than a high R score (sophisticated retention) — the relative importance
depends on the deployment context. The framework assumes that each dimension can be inde-
pendently assessed, which may not hold when dimensions interact (e.g., a higher R score may
increase C costs). We present DRICE as a structured vocabulary for cross-layer comparison,
not as a quantitative evaluation methodology.
    Several extensions could strengthen the framework. First, the E dimension (evidence depth)
currently uses our three-tier coding scheme; a more granular scale that captures the distinc-
tion between verified numbers and verified mechanisms would improve comparative precision.
Second, the C dimension aggregates four cost components; future work could weight these
components by their binding-constraint relevance in specific deployment scenarios. Third, the
framework does not capture temporal dynamics: a system’s DRICE profile may change as
workload patterns shift, memory pressure varies, or the diagnostic horizon extends. A dynamic
DRICE assessment that tracks profile changes over time would better capture the reality of
production deployments.
    The framework’s primary value lies in making implicit assumptions explicit. When a re-
searcher proposes a new diagnostic method, DRICE asks: what infrastructure assumptions does
it make (I)? When an infrastructure researcher designs a new eviction policy, DRICE asks: does
it consider diagnostic value (D)? This structured questioning is more actionable than a generic
call for “cross-layer optimization.”


9     Discussion
9.1    Key Patterns
Five patterns recur across the diagnostic and infrastructure layers:

The replay-accuracy trade-off is disappearing. Among the 12 failure-localization methods,
only two require environment replays (FAMAS, k=20; DoVer, 4–12 per case). The remaining

10 operate on single-trajectory analysis. MASPrism’s signal-extraction approach achieves com-
petitive accuracy with a 0.6B model and zero output tokens, at 6.69× lower latency than com-
parable methods. This trend suggests that the diagnostic layer’s demand on the infrastructure
layer is decreasing: newer methods require only logged traces, not re-execution access. The
implication for cross-layer design is significant — if future diagnostic methods universally op-
erate on logged traces stored independently of GPU memory, the Tier C evidence-loss scenario
becomes architecturally impossible.

Agent-aware infrastructure is emerging but single-objective. Nine of 22 infrastructure sys-
tems incorporate agent-level metadata into their management policies (ScaleSim, TokenCake,
KVFlow, KVCOMM, ForkKV, TokenDance, KAIROS, the policy-driven runtime, LRAgent).
However, all optimize for serving metrics (throughput, latency, cache hit rate) rather than di-
agnostic value. The agent-awareness is used to improve serving predictions, not to protect
diagnostic state. This creates an architectural asymmetry: the infrastructure layer is increas-
ingly capable of agent-level reasoning, but the objective function it optimizes does not include
diagnostic value.

The cross-layer conflict is narrower than assumed. The evidence-loss formalization (Eq. 1)
reveals that most diagnostic-serving interactions fall into Tier A (independent operation) or
Tier B (shared resource, reconstructable evidence). Tier C evidence-loss scenarios require non-
reconstructable state co-managed by the serving layer — a condition that current architectures
generally avoid by storing traces independently. Among 82 surveyed systems, we identified no
system where KV-cache eviction demonstrably destroyed diagnostic evidence in a production
deployment.

Step-level attribution remains an open challenge. Across the 12 failure-localization meth-
ods, step-level accuracy is substantially lower than agent-level accuracy. The best step-level re-
sults are approximately 52% (TrajAudit RootSE and CHIEF on algorithm-generated scenarios),
compared to agent-level accuracies exceeding 70%. Who&When reports step-level accuracy
degrades to approximately 0% on trajectories exceeding 93 steps. TraceElephant demonstrates
that complete traces improve step-level accuracy by 76% relative (16% to 28.1%), compared
to only 22% relative improvement at the agent level. This granularity gap implies that in-
frastructure decisions affecting trace completeness have a disproportionate impact on the most
challenging — and often most useful — level of diagnostic attribution.

Benchmark infrastructure is fragmented. The 12 surveyed benchmarks collectively cover
failure attribution, reliability, and safety evaluation, but use different trace formats, annotation
protocols, evaluation metrics, and failure definitions. No common evaluation protocol exists,
and 0 of 12 benchmarks evaluate diagnostic-infrastructure interaction. TraceElephant [6] pro-
vides the richest traces (380 structured JSONs with step-level tool logs) but no serving-side
instrumentation. Who&When [48] defines the first standardized attribution protocol but oper-
ates entirely offline. TRAIL [10] is the first OTel-format benchmark, enabling integration with
serving-side telemetry, but its best model achieves only 11% joint accuracy — suggesting that
structured traces alone are insufficient without specialized attribution methods. The absence of a
cross-layer benchmark is both a gap and an opportunity: building one would require combining
a realistic multi-agent workload generator, a configurable serving backend (with tunable evic-
tion policy and memory pressure), injected failures at known locations, and matched diagnostic
and serving metrics.

The subsampling paradox reveals heuristic limitations. The retention ablation results (Sec-
tion 7.3) demonstrate that heuristic attributors can improve when given less data — a finding
that challenges the intuition that more evidence is always better for diagnosis. The mechanism
is specific: the causal-graph heuristic marks an error as “recovered” if the same agent later pro-
duces a normal event, causing it to skip the true root cause on full traces where normal post-error
events are abundant. Subsampling strips these misleading normal events, making the decisive
error visible. This finding has two implications for cross-layer design: first, retention policies
that prioritize recency (keeping the most recent events) perform worst at low budgets because
they systematically discard early root causes. Second, the optimal retention strategy depends
on the attribution method — a finding that argues against one-size-fits-all retention policies and
for the retention-aware diagnostic design advocated in our validation protocol.

9.2    Implications for System Design
The patterns above suggest three concrete architectural recommendations:
  1. Decouple diagnostic storage from serving memory. Since 10 of 12 failure-localization
     methods operate on logged traces rather than live serving state, the simplest way to avoid
     Tier C evidence-loss events is to ensure traces are written to a durable store before the
     corresponding KV cache is eligible for eviction. This is already the default architecture
     in most surveyed systems, which is why the cross-layer conflict is rare in practice.
  2. Expose serving-side events to diagnostic consumers. The observability gap (no OTel
     attributes for KV cache residency, eviction events, or memory pressure) prevents diag-
     nostic systems from detecting when their evidence might be at risk. Extending the OTel
     GenAI conventions with serving-side attributes would enable diagnostic systems to re-
     quest trace retention proactively when they detect that a future attribution may be needed.
  3. Design retention policies with attribution method awareness. The subsampling para-
     dox shows that the same retention strategy can help one attribution method and hurt an-
     other. Future serving systems that incorporate diagnostic retention should parameterize
     the retention policy on the expected attribution method, or provide a general-purpose
     noise-filtering retention strategy that benefits multiple methods.

9.3    Limitations
This review has several limitations:
  1. Curated, not systematic. The corpus was assembled through iterative exploration and
      citation chaining rather than a pre-registered protocol with reproducible database queries.
      The reported counts are a curated landscape, not an exhaustive census.
  2. Evidence depth variation. Of the 60 entries, 56 received full-text verification (T1a or
      T1b), comprising 44 systems and all 12 benchmarks; the remaining 4 entries are discussed
      at the T2 text-mention level. Cross-system comparisons involving T2 systems should be
      interpreted with this asymmetry in mind.
  3. Retention ablation scope. The retention-ablation experiment uses real TraceElephant
      traces (220 failures, 5 task suites from 3 MAS frameworks) but tests only two heuristic at-
      tribution methods. LLM-judge approaches (CHIEF, TrajAudit, AgentRx) were not tested
      and may not exhibit the subsampling paradox, since they do not rely on the recovery-
      detection heuristic that drives the effect. Step-level attribution was not tested.
