                                              Abstract
         Foundation model-based multi-agent systems (MAS) introduce reliability requirements
     that span execution tracing, failure attribution, safety intervention, and serving-time re-
     source management. Existing reviews typically analyze these functions separately. This
     evidence-aware critical review maps 60 representative diagnostic methods, serving sys-
     tems, and evaluation benchmarks along explicit axes: required evidence inputs, replay and
     training demands, attribution granularity, intervention timing, state compatibility, work-
     load assumptions, and runtime cost. We distinguish functional roles from implementation
     strategies. A central question is when serving-state reclamation impairs later diagnosis.
     We show that evicting a KV cache is not equivalent to deleting a diagnostic trace: inter-
     ference arises only when evidence or a non-reconstructable execution dependency shares
     a constrained resource, is actually discarded, and is needed within the diagnostic hori-
     zon. We therefore formulate a conditional retention trade-off as a testable design hypoth-
     esis rather than an established universal conflict. We propose DRICE (Diagnostics evi-
     dence, Retention policy, Infrastructure assumptions, Cost decomposition, Evidence depth),
     a structured evaluation framework for cross-layer comparison. Finally, we outline an in-
     strumented, replay-capable evaluation protocol with matched reliability and serving met-
     rics, and report retention-ablation results on real multi-agent failure traces. The resulting
     synthesis highlights verifiable architectural interfaces and open questions without ranking
     non-comparable systems or claiming that a joint optimization has already been empirically
     demonstrated.

Keywords: Multi-agent systems, Foundation models, Failure localization, Observability, LLM
serving, KV-cache management

1     Introduction
Foundation model-based multi-agent systems (MAS), in which multiple Large Language Model
(LLM) agents collaborate to solve complex tasks, are increasingly moving from demonstrations
toward deployed workflows. As systems such as MetaGPT [13], CAMEL [15], ChatDev [30],
and Internet of Agents [7] operate within shared environments, reliability and efficiency de-
pend on operational layers that are less mature than orchestration itself. As these systems be-
come architecturally sophisticated, reliability and efficiency depend on operational layers that
lag behind the orchestration layer: the diagnostics layer, which supports failure localization,
observability, and safety intervention; and the infrastructure layer, which manages memory,
scheduling, and resources for multi-agent workloads.
    Recent multi-agent applications span collaborative coding, scientific discovery, financial
analysis, and social simulation. Within these applications, failure patterns are qualitatively
different from single-agent errors: MAST [5] identifies 14 recurring failure modes in MAS,
motivating runtime diagnostics rather than evaluation only at the final task output. When a
multi-agent workflow fails, the central question is not just what failed but which agent caused
the failure, at which step, and whether the serving infrastructure’s resource management deci-
sions contributed to or masked the failure. Recent preprints illustrate adjacent model-level prob-
lems that may arise in agent-enabled workloads: finite-beam reachability and continuous-query
generation in generative recommendation, attribution-based forgetting in LoRA adaptation, and
reading and reconciling temporal evidence in time-series forecasting. These are contextual ex-
amples rather than multi-agent diagnostic or serving systems, and are not included in our core
system tables.

1.1    Scope and Positioning
Existing surveys largely treat diagnostics and infrastructure separately. General agent surveys
cover architectural patterns and collaboration mechanisms; AgentOps addresses telemetry and
operational practice; offline agent evaluation benchmarks focus on task quality; single-model
serving surveys address latency and throughput optimization; and MAS collaboration/failure
surveys examine interaction patterns and failure modes. In our curated corpus, we review 48
entries across four categories (12 failure-localization, 6 observability, 8 safety, 22 infrastructure)
and 12 evaluation benchmarks (60 table entries; Appendix A). The resulting literature exposes
complementary pieces of the runtime stack, but the relationship between the state required for
diagnosis and the state managed by serving policies remains under-analyzed.
    This review examines the interface between these two selected runtime functions. The title
does not imply exhaustive treatment of orchestration, storage, networking, security operations,
or all layers of a production stack. Our analytical contribution is a state-dependency and cost
framework, not a claim that the reviewed mechanisms themselves are new. Table 1 contrasts
our scope against five adjacent review areas, specifying the conceptual dimension this review
adds to each.
    Three broad system properties recur across the literature reviewed here: (1) distributed
execution across multiple agents, which creates traces spanning agent boundaries; (2) inter-
agent communication, which allows failures to propagate through communication channels;
and (3) inter-agent memory sharing, where prompts, tools, and cached state may be reused
across the system. Section 5 derives serving-side implications from these properties, high-
lighting sparse activation, invocation distance, and inter-agent memory sharing as the workload
characteristics that directly affect memory placement, scheduling, and cache policy.

1.2    The Diagnostic-Infrastructure Gap
The gap between diagnostic and infrastructure research is both conceptual and practical. Con-
ceptually, diagnostic methods are designed under an implicit assumption that execution traces
are always available — stored in a log database, replay buffer, or trace store that is independent
of the serving engine’s memory management. Infrastructure systems, conversely, optimize for
throughput and latency without considering whether their eviction decisions affect downstream
diagnostic capability. Neither community has systematically characterized the conditions under
which these assumptions interact.
    Practically, this gap manifests in three ways. First, among the 12 evaluation benchmarks
in our curated corpus, we did not identify one that evaluates how diagnostic overhead affects
serving throughput or how eviction policies affect attribution accuracy. Second, within the
serving-infrastructure studies examined at full-text depth, we did not identify a reported sys-
tem that jointly optimizes diagnostic retention and serving throughput under a single measured
resource budget — the closest system (TOPAS) optimizes serving throughput alone. Third,
the observability standards that could bridge the gap (OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions) lack
serving-side attributes: there are no standardized attributes for KV cache residency, eviction
events, or memory pressure.
    This review addresses the gap by mapping both layers along shared dimensions (evidence
inputs, training, replay, granularity, timing, cost), formalizing the conditions for cross-layer
conflict (Section 6), and proposing a structured evaluation framework (DRICE, Section 8) and
validation protocol (Section 7.3) that make the shared-budget hypothesis testable.
    This is a curated critical review rather than a systematic review. The inclusion boundary,
evidence-selection rules, and verification tiers are described in Section 3. Quantitative claims
used for comparison come from full-text sources; abstract-level results are marked [a] and are
not used to compute cross-system deltas or rankings.

Why gap identification is a contribution. Identifying that a widely assumed conflict is con-
ditional (not universal) changes the design space for joint diagnostic-infrastructure systems. If
the conflict were universal, practitioners would need to build shared-budget managers for every
deployment. Because the conflict requires three specific conditions (Eq. 1), practitioners should
instead instrument for the specific conditions under which interference occurs. The simulated
KV-eviction experiment (Section 6.6) illustrates that, in a simplified CPU-based cache model,
naive policy augmentation produces a negative trade-off; live GPU-serving validation remains
future work. The retention ablation (Section 7.3) reveals a further non-obvious interaction on

2     Background
This section establishes the technical context for the diagnostic and infrastructure layers re-
viewed in subsequent sections. We describe the architecture of foundation model-based multi-
agent systems (§2.1), the workload characteristics that distinguish multi-agent serving from
single-model inference (§2.2), and the runtime layers we focus on (§2.3).

2.1    Foundation Model-Based Multi-Agent Systems
A foundation model-based agent operates in a perception–reasoning–action loop, using an LLM
to process observations and execute actions such as tool invocations, code execution, and inter-
agent communication. The underlying model workloads can also be multimodal: recent sys-
tems combine visual, audio, and clinical or platform metadata for domain-specific prediction
and governance. A multi-agent system (MAS) comprises multiple such agents, as exempli-
fied by MetaGPT, CAMEL, ChatDev, and Internet of Agents (IoA), operating within a shared
environment or communicating to accomplish collective objectives.
    Recent multi-agent applications also span reinforcement-learning-enhanced collaborative
reasoning and multimodal misinformation detection. MAST [5] identifies 14 recurring fail-
ure patterns in such systems, motivating runtime diagnostics rather than evaluation only at the
final task output. The rapid expansion of MAS into production deployments introduces op-
erational demands that simple orchestration frameworks do not address: when an agent fails
mid-workflow, which agent caused the failure? When a serving system evicts cached state,
does this affect later diagnostic capability? When a safety guardrail intercepts an action, does
the interception itself create a new failure mode?
    Three broad system properties recur across the literature: (1) distributed execution across
multiple agents, which creates execution traces spanning agent boundaries and introduces co-
ordination overhead; (2) inter-agent communication, which creates channels through which
failures can propagate (FAMAS’s spectrum analysis and TraceElephant’s trace collection both
exploit these channels for attribution); and (3) inter-agent memory sharing, where prompts,
tools, and cached state may be reused across the system, creating shared state dependencies that
affect both serving efficiency and diagnostic evidence availability.

2.2    Multi-Agent Workload Characteristics
Multi-agent workloads differ from single-model serving in three ways that directly affect mem-
ory placement, scheduling, and cache policy:

Sparse activation. Only a fraction of agents invoke the LLM at any moment: a 1,000-agent
simulation may have fewer than 10 actively generating tokens. Under such workloads, keeping
all agent state on the fastest memory tier can waste scarce accelerator memory. ScaleSim’s
invocation-distance metric quantifies the temporal gap before an agent’s next call, enabling
memory-tier demotion for inactive agents.

Invocation distance. Many workloads have structured activation patterns such as sequential
queues, event-driven messaging, and dependency graphs. The temporal gap before an agent’s
next call, termed invocation distance, can be estimated from application semantics in structured
workloads but is not generally visible to request-level schedulers. This motivates workflow-
aware caching approaches such as KVFlow, which predicts steps-to-execution for each cached
prefix.

Inter-agent memory sharing. Agents may share context such as system prompts, environ-
ment descriptions, and tool schemas. General serving systems already reuse such prefixes:
SGLang’s RadixAttention and vLLM’s automatic prefix caching both exploit shared token se-
quences. The additional opportunity in agentic systems is to use application-level structure
(dependency graphs, message queues, or execution order) to guide cache retention decisions
beyond request-level recency or frequency heuristics.
    The relative importance of these properties varies by application domain. Simulation work-
loads (ScaleSim’s target) exhibit strong sparse activation with predictable invocation patterns.
Coding agent workflows (Continuum’s target) have long tool-call pauses with uncertain du-
rations. Multi-model deployments (DroidSpeak’s target) add cross-architecture cache transfer
challenges. The infrastructure systems reviewed in Section 5 address different subsets of these
workload properties, and the optimal KV-cache management strategy depends on which prop-
erties dominate in a given deployment.

2.3    Runtime Layers
We focus on two runtime layers that are less mature than orchestration:

Diagnostics layer. This layer supports failure localization (identifying which agent or step
caused a failure), observability and tracing (continuously capturing execution evidence), and
safety intervention (actively monitoring and preventing harmful actions). The diagnostic layer
consumes execution state — traces, tool outputs, intermediate reasoning — to produce reliabil-
ity outcomes: attribution reports, anomaly alerts, and safety blocks. The surveyed methods vary
dramatically in their resource requirements: FAMAS requires 20 environment replays averag-
ing 105 minutes per task, while MASPrism requires only two deterministic prefill passes at 2.66
seconds on a 0.6B model. This three-orders-of-magnitude cost range has direct implications for
cross-layer design: methods at the high end of the cost spectrum may compete with serving
resources, while methods at the low end operate well within available headroom.

Infrastructure layer. This layer manages serving-time resources: KV-cache memory, com-
pute scheduling, adapter placement, and power management for multi-agent workloads. The
infrastructure layer consumes serving state — KV blocks, adapters, request metadata — and
optimizes for throughput, latency, and resource utilization. Among the 22 Tier-1 infrastructure

3     Review Methodology
We assembled a curated critical-review corpus through iterative database exploration, back-
ward/forward citation chaining, and eligibility-based selection. Because database-specific search
logs and screening decisions were not retained, we do not report a PRISMA-style numerical
flow or claim systematic completeness. The PRISMA 2020 guidelines [26] informed the re-
porting structure only. Figure 2 summarizes the sources, inclusion criteria, and final corpus
composition. We describe our search strategy, selection criteria, evidence-coding scheme, and
the classification taxonomy below.

3.1    Search Strategy
We conducted iterative searches across five academic databases: IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital
Library, Scopus, Google Scholar, and arXiv. The initial search window spanned January 2024
through March 2026, followed by a targeted citation-chaining update through August 2026, and
a final update pass through November 2026 capturing systems announced at NeurIPS 2025,
ICML 2026, ACL 2026, EMNLP 2026, OSDI 2024, FAST 2025, and EuroSys 2025/2027. Be-
cause foundation-model multi-agent runtime research is rapidly evolving, we included preprints
alongside peer-reviewed publications when the described system contained reproducible imple-
mentation details. We prioritize work from 2024–2026 because foundation-model multi-agent
runtime research is unusually recent, while retaining older serving papers when they define
mechanisms directly reused by agentic systems.
    We constructed Boolean search queries combining terms from two domains:
     • Diagnostics: (“multi-agent” OR “LLM agent” OR “foundation model agent”) AND
       (“failure localization” OR “fault attribution” OR “debugging” OR “observability” OR
       “tracing” OR “guardrail” OR “safety intervention” OR “runtime monitoring”)
     • Infrastructure: (“multi-agent” OR “LLM serving” OR “agent serving”) AND (“KV
       cache” OR “memory management” OR “scheduling” OR “state sharing” OR “cache evic-
       tion” OR “prefix caching” OR “context reuse”)
    Cross-domain queries additionally combined diagnostic and infrastructure terms to capture
systems at the intersection (e.g., “KV cache eviction” AND “diagnostic evidence”). To make the
review more reproducible without overstating exhaustiveness, candidate papers were collected
iteratively from primary systems papers, adjacent surveys, major peer-reviewed venues, and
forward/backward citation tracing. Queries were adapted per database syntax. A paper enters
the core system tables only when its runtime role can be identified from the source and mapped
to one of the functions above. Because the corpus was not generated by a preregistered database
protocol with fixed search strings and PRISMA-style screening, the reported counts should be
interpreted as a curated landscape rather than an exhaustive census.

Citation chaining. After the initial query-based search, we performed forward citation tracing
(finding papers that cite a seed paper) and backward citation tracing (examining references
within each selected paper). This process identified 34 additional failure-localization systems
not captured by the initial queries, primarily because they use domain-specific terminology
(“root cause analysis,” “trajectory debugging,” “error attribution”) that does not always co-
occur with “multi-agent” in titles or abstracts. We also traced infrastructure systems through
the KV-cache-management citation network starting from vLLM and SGLang, identifying 16
additional systems that address multi-agent workload patterns.

3.2    Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion criteria. A paper enters the core system tables when it satisfies at least one of the
following:
   1. It describes a runtime diagnostic operation for foundation-model agents: failure localiza-
      tion, observability or tracing, safety intervention, or diagnostic benchmarking.
   2. It describes a serving-time resource management mechanism (memory, scheduling, cache,
      or power) reused by or designed for agentic workloads.
   3. It provides a baseline or architectural prerequisite directly reused by multi-agent diagnos-
      tic or serving systems.

Exclusion criteria. We exclude: (a) orchestration-only frameworks without runtime diagnos-
tic or serving mechanisms (e.g., workflow graph definitions); (b) single-model inference op-
timization without multi-agent or agentic extensions; (c) offline-only evaluation methods that
do not address runtime behavior; (d) long-term memory architectures without serving-time re-
source management; and (e) general agent surveys that do not provide system-level implemen-
tation details for diagnosis or serving.

Selection cutoff. The reference list was frozen for editorial inspection on 7 November 2026.
This is a revision cutoff, not a systematically reconstructed search date: the original exploratory
collection did not preserve database-by-database queries, retrieval timestamps, or screening
exclusions. We therefore describe the corpus as a curated landscape rather than claiming
PRISMA-style systematic completeness.

3.3    Evidence Coding
Each included system is assigned an evidence tier that reflects the depth of our verification:
    • Tier 1a (T1a): Full-text reading with section-level evidence extraction. Quantitative
      claims, method details, cost metrics, and limitations are traced to specific sections, tables,
      or figures in the source paper. All systems in the core comparison tables are at least T1.
    • Tier 1b (T1b): Full-text reading but without page-level citation (e.g., the evidence was
      extracted from the complete paper but specific section numbers were not recorded).
    • Tier 2 (T2): Abstract-level verification. The system’s existence, claimed function, and
      headline results are confirmed, but detailed method mechanics and cost breakdowns are
      not independently verified.
    Abstract-only evidence is marked [a] in the text and is not used to compute cross-system
deltas or rankings. A source-level evidence-audit checklist accompanies the supplementary
material.
